Field test · Decision models

Jev, now with vision support: meet Clef, and watch it read documents

Jev brought decision models into the spotlight in September: a model that answers each question with a probability for every allowed answer, and writes no text. Jev reads text only. Two weeks later Cloudflare released Clef, a decision model built on the same idea that also accepts images. It has received far less attention. We tested it on documents.

October 2026Code, raw replies and research journal

The short version

  • Jev, now with vision support. Cloudflare's open Clef answers Jev-style questions about an image with a probability for every answer, and writes nothing.
  • Questions about the content: asked what a page says, Clef-flash scored __CORD_ACC__% on receipt totals and __DOC_ACC__% on questions about business pages.
  • It reads the page; it does not remember it. Asked to confirm a true total, it said yes __CR_REAL__% of the time with the receipt and __CR_BLANK__% with a blank page.
  • Questions about the document: asked what kind of page it was looking at, it scored __E01_ACC__% against the dataset's labels. Most of its misses were label problems, and its confident answers held up.
  • A larger model is not automatically safer. Clef 27B accepted __FOOLED_BIG__ receipt totals with one wrong digit; Clef-flash accepted __FOOLED_FLASH__.

What is Clef?

This is the introduction: the decision-model idea that Jev made popular, extended to images.

Most AI models in use today are generative: you ask, and they write an answer. That suits conversation, but much routine work is not conversation. When the question is is this an invoice? or is this the right total?, a written reply is slower and costlier than it needs to be, may arrive in an unexpected format, and gives no reliable signal of how sure the model is.

Decision models take a different approach. You send some input and a set of questions with fixed answers, and the model returns a probability for every allowed answer, for example for invoice, receipt and letter. Nothing is generated. Jev brought this approach to wide attention in September 2026, but Jev accepts text only.

On 1 October 2026 Cloudflare released Clef. It uses the same question types as Jev and accepts the same requests, its weights are open, and it comes in two sizes: Clef-flash (9 billion parameters) and Clef (27 billion). The difference is that Clef also accepts images. Cloudflare reports results on more than forty benchmarks, all of them text. None uses a document, and none reports whether the probabilities can be trusted.

What can a decision model do when it can see?

These are applications. Each one is a decision that depends on what a page or an image shows.

Document work is a long chain of small decisions, and they fall into two kinds. Some are about the document itself: what is this page, and is it complete? Others are about its content: what does it say, and is the extracted value right? A decision model that can see turns each of them into a single question about the page:

The same pattern applies beyond paperwork: is this screenshot an error screen, does this product photo match its listing, does this identity document show its photo page. We did not test those here.

Why not run OCR and send the text to Jev?

It is a fair question, since Jev already exists. For many of these decisions, text alone is not enough.

  • OCR keeps the words and drops the rest. Layout, ticks, stamps, signatures, handwriting, and which column a number sits in are lost or garbled on the way to text.
  • OCR mistakes become the model's facts. If OCR reads 30.000 as 30.900, a text model asked to check the extractor's value compares one reading of the page with another. Only a model that sees can check the value against the page itself.
  • Two systems instead of one. OCR and a text model must each be run, tuned and paid for, and their errors add up.

So the real question is whether a decision model that sees can read documents well enough to make these calls. That is what we tested.

We ran Clef-flash and Clef 27B on every test. Every question was asked twice: once as a multiple choice, and once as a plain yes or no. The wrong options were always realistic. On receipts they were the other amounts printed on the same receipt, plus the real total with one digit changed. On business pages they were real text from the same page. Part 1 · Questions about the content

Can it read what the page says?

This is the content test. Both models passed with scores we did not expect.

On receipts, Clef-flash picked the right total __CORD_RIGHT__ times out of __CORD_CHOICE_N__. On business pages it answered __DOC_RIGHT__ of __DOC_N__ multiple-choice questions correctly, and Clef 27B scored the same __DOC_BIG_ACC__%. What the page held made little difference:

Business pages: share of all questions (multiple choice and yes or no) answered correctly, by what the question needs.

The yes or no questions are stricter, because the wrong value can be almost right. Asked whether a receipt that says __NEAR_TRUE__ has a total of __NEAR_ASKED__, Clef-flash said no, __NEAR_NO__% sure. Across all __CORD_N__ receipts it accepted a changed digit only __FOOLED_FLASH__ time. Its confidence matched reality too: answers given with very high confidence were almost always right. That is what lets a machine handle the sure cases and a person the rest.

Clef 27B accepted __FOOLED_BIG__ wrong totals out of __FOOLED_N__; Clef-flash accepted __FOOLED_FLASH__. Almost all of the larger model's errors were near misses, within a few percent of the real total. Clef-flash behaves like a careful proof-reader that checks each digit; Clef 27B tends to accept a value that is approximately right. On business pages, where the wrong options are different text rather than near-miss numbers, the two models were level, and the larger model's few errors went the other way: it said no to __BIG_NO_TRUE__ answers that were right.

Checking extracted values is one of the most common tasks in document work, and for that task the smaller, faster and cheaper model was the safer one. The counts are small, so this is a strong indication rather than a proof.

Part 2 · The doubt

Is __DOC_ACC__% too good to be true?

This is a contamination check on the content results. Clef reads the page; it does not remember it.

Scores this high deserve scrutiny. All three test sets are public and have been online for years, so a model trained on web data may have seen these very pages. If so, part of the score would come from memory rather than reading.

We asked Clef-flash the same __CONTAM_CALLS__ questions again with only the image changed: once with a blank page of the same size, and once with another document from the same set. A model that reads should lose its accuracy. A model that remembers should keep it.

Clef-flash, the same questions with the real page, a blank page and another document's page. Dotted marks show chance for the multiple-choice questions.

The clearest measure is the yes or no question with the true value, because the question itself gives nothing away. With the real receipt, Clef-flash said yes __CR_REAL__% of the time; with a blank page, __CR_BLANK__%; with another receipt, __CR_SWAP__%. Business pages show the same pattern: __CD_REAL__%, then __CD_BLANK__% and __CD_SWAP__%. The model is reading the page.

On receipts, multiple choice without the page fell to __CRC_BLANK__% and __CRC_SWAP__%, below the __CRC_CHANCE__% that random guessing would give. On business pages it stayed at __CDC_BLANK__% with a blank page and __CDC_SWAP__% with another page, above the __CDC_CHANCE__% chance level. That is not memory. Some of our questions gave their answer away: which room, smoking or non-smoking? had only one option that is a type of room, and the corrected dinner time had only one evening time. Without the page the model answered __LEAK_WORDS__ word answers correctly, but only __LEAK_NUMS__ numbers. The business-page multiple-choice score therefore overstates the model slightly; the yes or no scores do not.

Part 3 · Questions about the document

Can it tell what kind of page it is?

This is the document test. The score looked weak until we checked the labels.

Clef-flash named the page type correctly __E01_ACC__% of the time, and Clef 27B __E02_ACC__%. Then we looked at the pages Clef-flash got wrong:

  • A page labelled letter is headed, in large type, Interoffice Memorandum. The model said memo.
  • A page labelled resume holds a single word: APPENDIX. The model said blank or unreadable.
  • A page labelled handwritten is a handwritten research report. The model said scientific report. Both are true.

To check this fairly, a separate reviewer looked at every missed page without seeing the model's answer. Of the __MISSES__ misses, __LABEL_PROBLEMS__ had a label problem: __LBL_WRONG__ labels were wrong, __LBL_NOTVIS__ could not be seen on the page at all, and __LBL_AMBIG__ were one of two equally true answers.

Clef-flash page-type answers on __E01_PAGES__ pages, grouped by its confidence. “Fits the page” means the blind reviewer listed the model's answer as a fair description of the page.

Of the __HIGH_N__ answers given with 70% confidence or more, __HIGH_WRONG__ were really wrong; the real errors all came with low confidence. With the checked labels the score rises to __E01_CHECKED__%, and to __E01_FITS__% if any answer that fits the page counts. Page types are harder than reading a value, but even here the confidence shows which answers can be trusted.

What tripped us up?

These are the practical points, for anyone who wants to try it.

  • Large images are refused. The hosted service estimates an image's size from its encoded text, so anything over about 185 KB is rejected, however simple the picture. We reduced pages to 1,024 pixels on the long side.
  • “Confidence” is not the probability of the answer. The hosted API's confidence field measures how concentrated all the probabilities are. Set thresholds on the probabilities themselves.
  • Test sets contain errors too. Old datasets carry wrong labels, and our own builder once offered “6-2-97” and “June 2, 1997” as two different answers. Read the failures before trusting a score.

What's next?

Each of these follows from something the results showed.

  • Harder content. Line items, counts, and whether the numbers on a page add up.
  • Real invoices. Business documents that are not well-known public test sets.
  • Tighter options. Wrong answers of the same kind as the right one, so that none can be guessed from the question.
  • The “approximately right” effect. More near-miss values, to see whether the larger model's tendency holds.

Notes on method

Both models on all three tests; the contamination check on Clef-flash only. Small samples: __E01_PAGES__ to __DOC_PAGES__ documents per test. Hosted models whose version cannot be pinned. Images were reduced to fit the size limit, and pages that still did not fit were left out. The page-type label review was done with AI help, blind to the model's answers, and still needs a final human pass. Every number and chart on this page is generated from the saved replies by docs/blog/build.py; the raw replies, the research journal and every script are in the doc-jev repository.
