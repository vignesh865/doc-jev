Field test · Decision models

Can Cloudflare's Clef read your documents without writing a word?

Everyone is talking about Jev. Two weeks later, almost nobody noticed that a decision model which can see had shipped. We pointed it at scans, receipts and business pages. It read them far better than we expected.

October 2026Code, raw replies and research journal

The short version

  • A vision decision model is here. Cloudflare's open Clef answers fixed-choice questions about an image with a probability for every answer, and writes nothing.
  • It reads content very well. 99% on receipt totals and 99% on questions about real business pages, out of the box.
  • Its confidence means something. On page types, every answer it gave with 70% confidence or more matched the label or fitted the page.
  • Bigger was not safer. The 27B model accepted 8 wrong totals that were off by one digit. The 9B model accepted 1.
  • We are not sure yet why it is this good. The test sets are public, so the model may have seen them. That test is next.

Wait, a decision model that can see?

This is the background: what a decision model is, and what Clef adds.

Most AI models you have used are talkers. You ask, they write. That is great for conversation and awkward for paperwork. When you only want to know is this an invoice? or is this the right total?, a written reply is slow, costs money, sometimes arrives in the wrong format, and gives no honest signal of how sure the model is.

Decision models skip the writing. You send some input and a few questions with fixed answers, and you get back a probability for every allowed answer, say for invoice, receipt and letter. Jev made the idea popular in September, but Jev reads text only.

On 1 October 2026 Cloudflare released two open models of the same kind, Clef (27 billion parameters) and the faster Clef-flash (9 billion), and these can look at images. Cloudflare's own results cover more than forty benchmarks, all text. None of them uses a document, and none says whether the probabilities can be trusted. So we tried.

Why point it at documents?

This is why the task fits. Most document work is deciding, not writing.

Behind every document pipeline sits a stream of small decisions. Each one maps straight onto a decision model's question types:

How did we test it?

This is the setup: three public document sets, two question styles, the model exactly as it ships.

  1. What kind of page is this? 60 scanned pages from RVL-CDIP, an old office archive with 16 page types (letter, memo, form, invoice, advert and so on). We added one more answer, blank or unreadable.
  2. What is the total on this receipt? 95 phone photos of shop receipts from CORD. The model had to pick the total from the other amounts printed on the same receipt, and to reject a total with one digit changed.
  3. What does this business page say? 120 pages of letters, forms, tables, charts and handwriting from DocVQA, with questions people wrote, such as What is the corrected dinner time? The wrong options were real text from the same page.

Each question was asked twice: once as a multiple choice, and once as a plain yes or no. Every correct answer comes from human labels. No training, no prompt tricks, the hosted model on Cloudflare. Here is one real call:

We send
{
  "model": "clef-flash",
  "images": [receipt photo],
  "state": "A photo of a shop receipt.",
  "questions": {
    "q": {
      "type": "noul",
      "instructions":
        "Is the total amount on this receipt 30.900?"
    }
  }
}
Clef answers
{
  "answers": {
    "q": { "type": "noul",
           "noul": 0.0132 }
  },
  "usage": { "input_tokens": 833,
             "output_tokens": 0 }
}

“noul” is the name for a yes or no question. The number is the probability of yes. Output tokens: zero, because nothing is written.

Can it read what's on the page?

This is the content test. It passed with a score we did not expect.

Clef-flash, share of multiple-choice questions answered correctly on each task.

On receipts, Clef-flash picked the right total 86 times out of 87. On business pages it got 118 of 119. Tables, forms, handwriting and charts all scored between 97% and 100%.

The yes or no questions are stricter, because the wrong value can be almost right. Asked whether a receipt that says 40.000 has a total of 40.001, it said no, 98% sure. Across all 95 receipts it fell for a changed digit only 1 time.

Its confidence also matched reality: answers given with very high confidence were almost always right. That is what lets you hand the sure cases to a machine and the rest to a person.

Is 99% too good to be true?

This is a contamination question, and we have not answered it yet.

All three test sets are public and have been on the internet for years. A model trained on a large slice of the web may have seen these very pages, or at least pages very like them. If so, part of the score is memory, not reading.

The test is simple, and it is the first thing we will run next: ask the same questions with a blank image, and with another receipt's image. A model that reads the page should collapse to guessing. A model that remembers should not. We ran out of free daily quota before we could do it, so treat the content scores as a strong first result, not a final one.

Why did page types score only 57%?

This is a label check. Many of the “mistakes” were not mistakes.

The page-type test looked weak at first. Then we looked at the pages it got wrong:

  • A page labelled letter is headed, in large type, Interoffice Memorandum. The model said memo.
  • A page labelled resume holds a single word: APPENDIX. The model said blank or unreadable.
  • A page labelled handwritten is a handwritten research report. The model said scientific report. Both are true.

To check this fairly, a separate reviewer looked at every missed page without seeing the model's answer. Of the 26 misses, 11 had a label problem: 4 labels were wrong, 4 could not be seen on the page at all, and 3 were one of two equally true answers.

Page-type answers on 60 pages, grouped by the model's confidence. “Fits the page” means the blind reviewer listed the model's answer as a fair description of the page.

The pattern is the point. Of the 39 answers given with 70% confidence or more, 0 were really wrong. The real errors all came with low confidence. With the checked labels the score rises to 68%, and to 82% if any answer that fits the page counts.

Is the bigger model safer?

This is the 27B comparison. On the strictest test, it was worse.

We ran the page-type and receipt tests again with the larger Clef. It chose a little better: 63% against 57% on page types, and 100% on receipt totals. Then came the one-digit test:

“Is the total amount on this receipt 30.900?”
Clef-flashno, 99%

Receipts: how often each model said yes to a total with one digit changed, by how far that total was from the real one.

The big model said yes to 8 wrong totals out of 95. Clef-flash said yes to 1. Almost all of the big model's slips were near misses, within a few percent of the real total. Clef-flash behaves like a proof-reader checking digit by digit. The big model behaves more like someone who glances and thinks “close enough”.

Checking extracted values is one of the most common jobs in document work, and there the smaller, faster, cheaper model was the safer one. The counts are small, so read this as a strong hint.

What tripped us up?

These are the practical surprises, for anyone who wants to try it.

  • Big images are refused. The hosted service guesses an image's size from its encoded text, so anything over about 185 KB is rejected, however simple the picture. We shrank pages to 1,024 pixels on the long side.
  • “Confidence” is not what you think. The hosted API's confidence field is not the probability of the chosen answer. It measures how bunched up all the probabilities are. Set thresholds on the probabilities.
  • Test sets have mistakes too. Old datasets carry wrong labels, and our own builder once offered “6-2-97” and “June 2, 1997” as two different answers. Read the failures before you trust a score.

What's next?

Each of these follows from something the results showed.

  • The contamination test. Blank and swapped images, to see how much of the score is reading and how much is memory.
  • Harder content. Line items, counts, and whether the numbers on a page add up.
  • Real invoices. Business documents that are not famous public test sets.
  • The “close enough” effect. More near-miss values, to see whether the bigger model's habit holds up.

Notes on method

Clef-flash on all three sets; Clef 27B on page types and receipts only. Small samples: 60 to 120 documents per set. Hosted models whose version cannot be pinned. Images were reduced to fit the size limit, and pages that still did not fit were left out. The page-type label review was done with AI help, blind to the model's answers, and still needs a final human pass. Every number and chart on this page is generated from the saved replies by docs/blog/build.py; the raw replies, the research journal and every script are in the doc-jev repository.
