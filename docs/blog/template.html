Field test · Decision models

Jev, now with vision support: meet Clef, and watch it read documents

Jev brought decision models into the spotlight in September: a model that answers each question with a probability for every allowed answer, and writes no text. Jev reads text only. Two weeks later Cloudflare released Clef, a decision model built on the same idea that also accepts images. It has received far less attention. We tested it on documents.

October 2026Code, raw replies and research journal

The short version

  • Jev, now with vision support. Cloudflare's open Clef answers Jev-style questions about an image with a probability for every answer, and writes nothing.
  • About the document: asked what kind of page it was looking at, it scored __E01_ACC__% against the dataset's labels. Most of its “mistakes” were label problems, and its confident answers held up.
  • About the content: asked what the page says, it scored __CORD_ACC__% on receipt totals and __DOC_ACC__% on questions about business pages.
  • It is reading, not remembering. Asked to confirm a true value, it said yes __CR_REAL__% of the time with the receipt and __CR_BLANK__% with a blank page.
  • Bigger was not safer. The 27B model accepted __FOOLED_BIG__ receipt totals with one wrong digit. The 9B model accepted __FOOLED_FLASH__.

What is Clef?

This is the introduction: the decision-model idea that Jev made popular, extended to images.

Most AI models in use today are generative: you ask, and they write an answer. That suits conversation, but much routine work is not conversation. When the question is is this an invoice? or is this the right total?, a written reply is slower and costlier than it needs to be, may arrive in an unexpected format, and gives no reliable signal of how sure the model is.

Decision models take a different approach. You send some input and a set of questions with fixed answers, and the model returns a probability for every allowed answer, for example for invoice, receipt and letter. Nothing is generated. Jev brought this approach to wide attention in September 2026, but Jev accepts text only.

On 1 October 2026 Cloudflare released Clef. It uses the same question types as Jev and accepts the same requests, its weights are open, and it comes in two sizes: Clef-flash (9 billion parameters) and Clef (27 billion). The difference is that Clef also accepts images. Cloudflare reports results on more than forty benchmarks, all of them text. None uses a document, and none reports whether the probabilities can be trusted.

What changes when a decision model can see?

This is why vision matters. The decision can be made from the page itself.

  • No text extraction step. A text-only model needs the page converted to text first, usually by OCR. Each conversion loses something: layout, table structure, ticks, stamps, handwriting. A model that sees decides from the page as it is.
  • Questions text cannot carry. Is the form signed? Is the box ticked? Is the page blank? Is this a scan or a photograph? These depend on what the page looks like, not only on its words.
  • One request per page. The image goes in, typed answers come out, and each answer can drive an action directly: route the document, accept a value, or send the page to a person.
  • Beyond documents. The same applies to screenshots, product photos and identity documents. We did not test those here.

Why point it at documents?

This is why the task fits. Most document work is deciding, not writing.

Behind every document pipeline sits a stream of small decisions, and they come in two kinds. Some are about the document itself: what is this page? Others are about its content: what does it say, and is the extracted value right? Each one maps straight onto a decision model's question types:

We ran both Clef-flash and Clef 27B on every test. Every question was asked twice: once as a multiple choice, and once as a plain yes or no. The wrong options were always realistic. On receipts they were the other amounts printed on the same receipt, plus the real total with one digit changed. On business pages they were real text from the same page. Part 1 · About the document

Can it tell what kind of page it is?

This is the document test. The score looked weak, until we checked the labels.

Clef-flash named the page type correctly __E01_ACC__% of the time, and Clef 27B __E02_ACC__%. Then we looked at the pages Clef-flash got wrong:

  • A page labelled letter is headed, in large type, Interoffice Memorandum. The model said memo.
  • A page labelled resume holds a single word: APPENDIX. The model said blank or unreadable.
  • A page labelled handwritten is a handwritten research report. The model said scientific report. Both are true.

To check this fairly, a separate reviewer looked at every missed page without seeing the model's answer. Of the __MISSES__ misses, __LABEL_PROBLEMS__ had a label problem: __LBL_WRONG__ labels were wrong, __LBL_NOTVIS__ could not be seen on the page at all, and __LBL_AMBIG__ were one of two equally true answers.

Clef-flash page-type answers on __E01_PAGES__ pages, grouped by its confidence. “Fits the page” means the blind reviewer listed the model's answer as a fair description of the page.

The pattern is the point. Of the __HIGH_N__ answers given with 70% confidence or more, __HIGH_WRONG__ were really wrong. The real errors all came with low confidence. With the checked labels the score rises to __E01_CHECKED__%, and to __E01_FITS__% if any answer that fits the page counts. Even where the headline number looks weak, the confidence tells you which answers to trust.

Part 2 · About the content

Can it read what the page says?

This is the content test. Both models passed with scores we did not expect.

On receipts, Clef-flash picked the right total __CORD_RIGHT__ times out of __CORD_CHOICE_N__. On business pages it answered __DOC_RIGHT__ of __DOC_N__ multiple-choice questions correctly, and Clef 27B scored the same __DOC_BIG_ACC__%. It did not matter much what the page held:

Business pages: share of all questions (multiple choice and yes or no) answered correctly, by what the question needs.

The yes or no questions are stricter, because the wrong value can be almost right. Asked whether a receipt that says __NEAR_TRUE__ has a total of __NEAR_ASKED__, Clef-flash said no, __NEAR_NO__% sure. Across all __CORD_N__ receipts it fell for a changed digit only __FOOLED_FLASH__ time. Its confidence matched reality too: answers given with very high confidence were almost always right. That is what lets you hand the sure cases to a machine and the rest to a person.

The big model said yes to __FOOLED_BIG__ wrong totals out of __FOOLED_N__. Clef-flash said yes to __FOOLED_FLASH__. Almost all of the big model's slips were near misses, within a few percent of the real total. Clef-flash behaves like a proof-reader checking digit by digit. The big model behaves more like someone who glances and thinks “close enough”. On business pages, where the wrong options are different text rather than near-miss numbers, the two were level; there the big model's few errors went the other way, saying no to __BIG_NO_TRUE__ answers that were right.

Checking extracted values is one of the most common jobs in document work, and there the smaller, faster, cheaper model was the safer one. The counts are small, so read this as a strong hint.

Part 3 · The doubt

Is __DOC_ACC__% too good to be true?

This is a contamination check. It is reading the page, not remembering it.

The content scores made us suspicious. All three test sets are public and have been online for years, so a model trained on the web may have seen these very pages. If so, part of the score would be memory.

So we asked Clef-flash the same __CONTAM_CALLS__ questions again, with only the picture changed: once with a blank page of the same size, and once with another document from the same set. A model that reads should fall apart. A model that remembers should not.

Clef-flash, the same questions with the real page, a blank page and another document's page. Dotted marks show chance for the multiple-choice questions.

The cleanest test is the yes or no question with the true value, because the question gives nothing away. With the real receipt, Clef-flash said yes __CR_REAL__% of the time. With a blank page, __CR_BLANK__%. With another receipt, __CR_SWAP__%. Business pages told the same story: __CD_REAL__%, then __CD_BLANK__% and __CD_SWAP__%. It is reading the page.

On receipts, multiple choice without the page fell to __CRC_BLANK__% and __CRC_SWAP__%, below the __CRC_CHANCE__% you would get by guessing. On business pages it stayed at __CDC_BLANK__% with a blank page and __CDC_SWAP__% with another page, well above the __CDC_CHANCE__% chance level. That is not memory. Some of our questions simply gave their answer away: which room, smoking or non-smoking? with only one option that is a room type, or the corrected dinner time with only one evening time. Without the page it got __LEAK_WORDS__ word answers right, but only __LEAK_NUMS__ numbers. So the business-page multiple-choice score flatters it a little; the yes or no scores do not.

What tripped us up?

These are the practical surprises, for anyone who wants to try it.

  • Big images are refused. The hosted service guesses an image's size from its encoded text, so anything over about 185 KB is rejected, however simple the picture. We shrank pages to 1,024 pixels on the long side.
  • “Confidence” is not what you think. The hosted API's confidence field is not the probability of the chosen answer. It measures how bunched up all the probabilities are. Set thresholds on the probabilities.
  • Test sets have mistakes too. Old datasets carry wrong labels, and our own builder once offered “6-2-97” and “June 2, 1997” as two different answers. Read the failures before you trust a score.

What's next?

Each of these follows from something the results showed.

  • Harder content. Line items, counts, and whether the numbers on a page add up.
  • Real invoices. Business documents that are not famous public test sets.
  • Tighter options. Wrong answers that are the same kind of thing as the right one, so none can be guessed from the question.
  • The “close enough” effect. More near-miss values, to see whether the bigger model's habit holds up.

Notes on method

Both models on all three tests; the contamination check on Clef-flash only. Small samples: __E01_PAGES__ to __DOC_PAGES__ documents per test. Hosted models whose version cannot be pinned. Images were reduced to fit the size limit, and pages that still did not fit were left out. The page-type label review was done with AI help, blind to the model's answers, and still needs a final human pass. Every number and chart on this page is generated from the saved replies by docs/blog/build.py; the raw replies, the research journal and every script are in the doc-jev repository.
