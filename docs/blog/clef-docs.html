Field test · Decision models

Can Cloudflare's Clef read your documents without writing a word?

Everyone is talking about Jev. Two weeks later, almost nobody noticed that a decision model which can see had shipped. We pointed it at scans, receipts and business pages. It read them so well that we went looking for the trick.

October 2026Code, raw replies and research journal

The short version

  • A vision decision model is here. Cloudflare's open Clef answers fixed-choice questions about an image with a probability for every answer, and writes nothing.
  • About the document: asked what kind of page it was looking at, it scored 57% against the dataset's labels. Most of its “mistakes” were label problems, and its confident answers held up.
  • About the content: asked what the page says, it scored 99% on receipt totals and 99% on questions about business pages.
  • It is reading, not remembering. Asked to confirm a true value, it said yes 100% of the time with the receipt and 0% with a blank page.
  • Bigger was not safer. The 27B model accepted 8 receipt totals with one wrong digit. The 9B model accepted 1.

Wait, a decision model that can see?

This is the background: what a decision model is, and what Clef adds.

Most AI models you have used are talkers. You ask, they write. That is great for conversation and awkward for paperwork. When you only want to know is this an invoice? or is this the right total?, a written reply is slow, costs money, sometimes arrives in the wrong format, and gives no honest signal of how sure the model is.

Decision models skip the writing. You send some input and a few questions with fixed answers, and you get back a probability for every allowed answer, say for invoice, receipt and letter. Jev made the idea popular in September, but Jev reads text only.

On 1 October 2026 Cloudflare released two open models of the same kind, Clef (27 billion parameters) and the faster Clef-flash (9 billion), and these can look at images. Cloudflare's own results cover more than forty benchmarks, all text. None of them uses a document, and none says whether the probabilities can be trusted. So we tried.

Why point it at documents?

This is why the task fits. Most document work is deciding, not writing.

Behind every document pipeline sits a stream of small decisions, and they come in two kinds. Some are about the document itself: what is this page? Others are about its content: what does it say, and is the extracted value right? Each one maps straight onto a decision model's question types:

We ran both Clef-flash and Clef 27B on every test. Every question was asked twice: once as a multiple choice, and once as a plain yes or no. The wrong options were always realistic. On receipts they were the other amounts printed on the same receipt, plus the real total with one digit changed. On business pages they were real text from the same page. Every correct answer comes from human labels. No training and no prompt tricks. Here is one real call:

Part 1 · About the document

Can it tell what kind of page it is?

This is the document test. The score looked weak, until we checked the labels.

Clef-flash named the page type correctly 57% of the time, and Clef 27B 63%. Then we looked at the pages Clef-flash got wrong:

  • A page labelled letter is headed, in large type, Interoffice Memorandum. The model said memo.
  • A page labelled resume holds a single word: APPENDIX. The model said blank or unreadable.
  • A page labelled handwritten is a handwritten research report. The model said scientific report. Both are true.

To check this fairly, a separate reviewer looked at every missed page without seeing the model's answer. Of the 26 misses, 11 had a label problem: 4 labels were wrong, 4 could not be seen on the page at all, and 3 were one of two equally true answers.

Clef-flash page-type answers on 60 pages, grouped by its confidence. “Fits the page” means the blind reviewer listed the model's answer as a fair description of the page.

The pattern is the point. Of the 39 answers given with 70% confidence or more, 0 were really wrong. The real errors all came with low confidence. With the checked labels the score rises to 68%, and to 82% if any answer that fits the page counts. Even where the headline number looks weak, the confidence tells you which answers to trust.

Part 2 · About the content

Can it read what the page says?

This is the content test. Both models passed with scores we did not expect.

On receipts, Clef-flash picked the right total 86 times out of 87. On business pages it answered 118 of 119 multiple-choice questions correctly, and Clef 27B scored the same 99%. It did not matter much what the page held:

Business pages: share of all questions (multiple choice and yes or no) answered correctly, by what the question needs.

The yes or no questions are stricter, because the wrong value can be almost right. Asked whether a receipt that says 40.000 has a total of 40.001, Clef-flash said no, 98% sure. Across all 95 receipts it fell for a changed digit only 1 time. Its confidence matched reality too: answers given with very high confidence were almost always right. That is what lets you hand the sure cases to a machine and the rest to a person.

The big model said yes to 8 wrong totals out of 95. Clef-flash said yes to 1. Almost all of the big model's slips were near misses, within a few percent of the real total. Clef-flash behaves like a proof-reader checking digit by digit. The big model behaves more like someone who glances and thinks “close enough”. On business pages, where the wrong options are different text rather than near-miss numbers, the two were level; there the big model's few errors went the other way, saying no to 4 answers that were right.

Checking extracted values is one of the most common jobs in document work, and there the smaller, faster, cheaper model was the safer one. The counts are small, so read this as a strong hint.

Part 3 · The doubt

Is 99% too good to be true?

This is a contamination check. It is reading the page, not remembering it.

The content scores made us suspicious. All three test sets are public and have been online for years, so a model trained on the web may have seen these very pages. If so, part of the score would be memory.

So we asked Clef-flash the same 1,274 questions again, with only the picture changed: once with a blank page of the same size, and once with another document from the same set. A model that reads should fall apart. A model that remembers should not.

Clef-flash, the same questions with the real page, a blank page and another document's page. Dotted marks show chance for the multiple-choice questions.

The cleanest test is the yes or no question with the true value, because the question gives nothing away. With the real receipt, Clef-flash said yes 100% of the time. With a blank page, 0%. With another receipt, 0%. Business pages told the same story: 98%, then 7% and 2%. It is reading the page.

On receipts, multiple choice without the page fell to 18% and 25%, below the 28% you would get by guessing. On business pages it stayed at 55% with a blank page and 44% with another page, well above the 29% chance level. That is not memory. Some of our questions simply gave their answer away: which room, smoking or non-smoking? with only one option that is a room type, or the corrected dinner time with only one evening time. Without the page it got 39 of 63 word answers right, but only 19 of 44 numbers. So the business-page multiple-choice score flatters it a little; the yes or no scores do not.

What tripped us up?

These are the practical surprises, for anyone who wants to try it.

  • Big images are refused. The hosted service guesses an image's size from its encoded text, so anything over about 185 KB is rejected, however simple the picture. We shrank pages to 1,024 pixels on the long side.
  • “Confidence” is not what you think. The hosted API's confidence field is not the probability of the chosen answer. It measures how bunched up all the probabilities are. Set thresholds on the probabilities.
  • Test sets have mistakes too. Old datasets carry wrong labels, and our own builder once offered “6-2-97” and “June 2, 1997” as two different answers. Read the failures before you trust a score.

What's next?

Each of these follows from something the results showed.

  • Harder content. Line items, counts, and whether the numbers on a page add up.
  • Real invoices. Business documents that are not famous public test sets.
  • Tighter options. Wrong answers that are the same kind of thing as the right one, so none can be guessed from the question.
  • The “close enough” effect. More near-miss values, to see whether the bigger model's habit holds up.

Notes on method

Both models on all three tests; the contamination check on Clef-flash only. Small samples: 60 to 120 documents per test. Hosted models whose version cannot be pinned. Images were reduced to fit the size limit, and pages that still did not fit were left out. The page-type label review was done with AI help, blind to the model's answers, and still needs a final human pass. Every number and chart on this page is generated from the saved replies by docs/blog/build.py; the raw replies, the research journal and every script are in the doc-jev repository.
